For HR, an open role is a process problem. For Finance, it's a cost line that almost never shows up in any report. That's the blind spot: a headhunter's fee is visible, approved and audited. The cost of leaving the seat empty for three months is not.

## Why "cost-per-hire" isn't enough

The metric Finance usually looks at is how much it costs to hire: the agency fee, the paid job post, the interview hours. It's a real metric, but it measures only one part of the problem — and almost always the smallest one.

What's missing from that calculation is the **cost of vacancy**: what it costs the company for that role to remain unfilled. For a key tech position (a tech lead, a data role that's critical to the roadmap, a role that blocks another team), that cost usually far exceeds the hiring fee.

## The framework: cost of vacancy

The logic is simple, even though the exact number varies by company and by role:

**Cost of vacancy = (estimated daily value of the role) × (days the role stays open)**

The "daily value of the role" is not just the salary that isn't being paid — in fact, that's a saving, not a cost. It's the combination of:

- **Lost productivity:** features that don't ship, projects that fall behind, technical debt that piles up because nobody is addressing it.
- **Overload on the existing team:** overtime, burnout, and the risk that someone else quits from covering two roles.
- **Opportunity cost:** initiatives that depended on that role and are now on hold.
- **Rework once the role is finally filled:** onboarding, the learning curve, and sometimes fixing decisions made without that technical judgment at the table.

None of these items show up on an invoice. That's why Finance tends to systematically underestimate them compared with the hiring fee, which does arrive as a round number in an email.

## An illustrative example (adjust it with your own numbers)

| | Role filled in 4 weeks | Role filled in 12 weeks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hiring cost (fee) | Same in both cases | Same in both cases |
| Weeks of lost productivity | 4 | 12 |
| Team overload | Low | High, with attrition risk |
| Blocked projects | 0–1 | 2–3 |

With this blank table, any Finance team can fill in its own values (the role's fully loaded salary, the value of the project that depends on it, the cost of overtime) and arrive at its own number. The point isn't the exact figure: it's that, almost always, filling a role fast is cheaper than filling it slowly, even if the fee for fast hiring looks "more expensive" at first glance.

## How this translates into a buying decision

When Finance evaluates whether it's worth paying for proactive sourcing (a headhunter) instead of waiting for the right candidate to arrive through a job board:

1. **Calculate the weekly cost of vacancy** for the specific role, not a generic company-wide average.
2. **Compare that number against the time difference** between an in-house search and a managed one (for scarce tech roles, the difference is usually several weeks — we covered it in [how long it takes to fill a tech role](/en/blog/cuanto-tarda-cubrir-vacante-tech)).
3. If the weekly cost of vacancy exceeds the difference in fees, **the fast search has already paid for itself before it starts**.

## The question that changes the conversation

Instead of "how much does it cost to hire?", the question that serves Finance is: **"how much does it cost us not to have this person working?"** That reframing is what turns a discussion about expenses into a business decision.
